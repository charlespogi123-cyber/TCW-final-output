The issue

AI runs on data centres, and data centres are being built wherever power, land, and policy allow. The Philippines wants a share: the DICT plans to grow AI-oriented capacity from about 50 megawatts to 1.5 gigawatts by 2033, with an estimated US$34.4 billion in investment (DICT, 2026; PNA, 2026). That is globalization in a concrete form: foreign capital, cloud demand, and a technology supply chain meeting a local grid and local water.

The benefits are projections. The costs are physical. Large facilities need steady electricity and cooling, and where cooling uses water, they compete with households and farms. The question this paper answers is practical: how can the Philippines welcome the investment while making sure power, water, and host communities are protected?

What the evidence shows

Three findings from elsewhere in Southeast Asia and Europe frame the Philippine choice. First, demand can concentrate fast. In Ireland, data centres in the Dublin and Meath region account for close to half of local electricity demand, which led to an effective pause on new grid connections (Mason Hayes & Curran, 2026). Second, water becomes a bottleneck before it becomes a headline. Malaysia’s water regulator reported that data centres in Johor, Selangor, and Negeri Sembilan requested 808 million litres a day in 2024, and only 142.06 million litres a day were approved (Free Malaysia Today, 2025). Third, efficiency is a policy choice, not an accident. Singapore pays for scarce new capacity with strict conditions, which I compare below.

What this does and does not prove. These cases show that data-centre growth can strain grids and water, and that governments can set conditions. They do not prove the same outcome in the Philippines. Local data on each site is still needed.

None of these thresholds should be copied number for number. Singapore has no large farming sector and Ireland has a very different grid. What transfers is the mechanism: conditions first, capacity second.

The root problem: rules that arrive after the buildings

The Philippine plan sets a capacity target, but the cases above show that results depend on rules written before construction. I define the root problem as a governance and information gap: cooling technology and clean-power options exist, yet I found no single published rule that ties capacity to verified water and power performance. If such a rule exists, this gap narrows. Five pieces of missing information matter most, and each has evidence behind it:

  • Facility-level water balance. In Malaysia, requests for 808 million litres a day were cut to 142.06 million approved (Free Malaysia Today, 2025), so planned demand and available water can diverge. DICT says modern data centres use water-recycling cooling (GMA News Online, 2026), but that is a design claim, not a site result.
  • Combined load in one corridor. Data centres in Dublin and Meath account for close to half of regional electricity demand (Mason Hayes & Curran, 2026). DICT says its AI corridors were mapped to avoid crowding residents (Bilyonaryo News Channel, 2026), but the combined load of several campuses is not yet published.
  • Effects on host communities. Philippine reporting has raised concerns about energy-burdened households (Earth Journalism Network, 2026).
  • Actual renewable share. Some projects plan renewable-powered data centres (Philstar.com, 2026), while Ireland requires 80% renewable procurement for new connections (Mason Hayes & Curran, 2026). A per-facility Philippine share is not yet set.
  • Who acts when plans fail. Johor coordinates one review across 20 agencies (New Straits Times, 2025). I found no equivalent single review for Philippine data centres.

My solution: three safeguards, one feedback loop

The three safeguards close the gaps above, and they are built to work together.

11. Meaningful participation of communities and local governments

Host communities and LGUs get plain-language information on expected water and power use before approval, a defined way to raise concerns, and a written response on record. This continues through construction and operation.

22. Project-specific assessment and continuous monitoring

Each campus is assessed for water source and balance, power demand and renewable share, heat, land use, and combined effects with nearby sites. Baselines come before construction; actual use is compared with the plan, and large gaps trigger corrective action.

33. Facility-level water, energy, and renewable requirements

Before operating, each facility files a plan naming its water source (reclaimed or non-potable first, as in Johor), design targets for PUE and water-use effectiveness, and its contracted renewable share. Targets cover both the share recycled and total freshwater used, and are set per site, not copied from abroad.

How they connect

Enforcement. To give the loop teeth, capacity is released in tranches (for example, 100 MW at a time). The next tranche opens only after the previous one meets its published conditions, much as Singapore awards new capacity by competitive call (Conventus Law, n.d.).

Trade-off. Water, energy, and renewable requirements rank first because they act directly on the resource problem and keep working over time, but they need a legal basis and local calibration. Participation is the easiest to start. Holding back a tranche is the strongest check on accountability and the hardest to carry out, which is why it depends on conditions published in advance.

Johor’s single review across 20 agencies shows this coordination is possible but demanding (New Straits Times, 2025).

Limits of this analysis

This paper relies on published reporting and policy documents. I did not find Philippine site-level water and power data for the planned campuses, so the thresholds proposed here (PUE, renewable share, tranche size) are starting points to be calibrated with DICT, DOE, and DENR. The comparisons draw on Singapore, Ireland, and Malaysia, whose grids, water systems, and laws differ from the Philippines. I could not confirm whether existing Philippine law already covers some of these requirements, which is where a legal review would begin. Foreign rules are summarised from law-firm and news reports, so the next check is the regulators’ own documents (CRU, IMDA, SPAN).
